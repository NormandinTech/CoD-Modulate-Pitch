Where credit is due

The detection layer works

Let's start with something most critiques of Call of Duty's moderation won't say: the detection layer works.

Since Activision deployed Modulate's ToxMod in 2023, the published numbers are real and meaningful. Exposure to disruptive voice chat dropped roughly 43–50% depending on the reporting period. Repeat offenses fell sharply after enforcement improvements in mid-2024 — Activision reported that 80% of players who received a voice-chat enforcement did not re-offend. Millions of accounts have been actioned under the Code of Conduct, and every flagged incident is reviewed by a human before enforcement. Activision has also published multiple transparency reports on this data, which is more than most of the industry can say.

That's the part that's solved. AI can now hear harassment at CoD scale, distinguish it (imperfectly) from banter, and route it to humans. Five years ago that was science fiction.

So why do players still hate the system?

Because detection was never the whole problem. The system catches toxicity, but it hasn't earned trust. Ask any CoD community — Reddit, Discord, your own lobby — and you'll hear the same complaints, and they're not "toxicity is still bad." They're:

  • "I got restricted and I don't know what I said."
  • "My friends and I talk trash to each other. That's the game. Now we self-censor in our own party."
  • "I appealed and got a form response."
  • "I got flagged once two years ago and I feel like the system still has it out for me."

Notice that none of these are detection failures. They're procedural failures — failures of transparency, evidence, context, and forgiveness. The AI got smarter; the process around it stayed a black box.

Read that first number from the player's side. When players participate in moderation, three-quarters of the time nothing they submit can even be evaluated — and when moderation acts on them, they're shown nothing at all. The evidence asymmetry runs entirely one direction. That asymmetry is the trust problem, and it's fixable.

What follows is a design proposal: six principles, an architecture that implements them, and an honest accounting of the tradeoffs. None of it requires new AI breakthroughs. All of it is process and product design layered on detection capabilities that already exist.

The framework

Six principles for a system players can trust

Principle 01 — Clear rulesTransparency instead of mystery: publish the actual rules

The Code of Conduct says harassment isn't tolerated. Players acknowledge it at first launch. But there's a canyon between "harassment will not be tolerated" and knowing where the line actually sits in a game whose culture includes competitive trash talk by design — something Activision itself acknowledges ("hype and passion is part of Call of Duty's DNA").

The fix is a public rules document with examples and edge cases: here's banter that's fine, here's the point where it becomes targeting, here's what escalates a mute to a ban. Riot and Xbox have both moved toward behavioral specificity in their published standards; nothing about specificity helps bad actors more than it helps the honest majority. People follow rules they can actually see.

Design rule: if a behavior can trigger enforcement, a player should be able to find a written, concrete description of it before it happens — not after.

Principle 02 — Logged incidentsEvidence instead of vibes: show the clip

Today, ToxMod flags incidents, a human reviews them, and enforcement lands on the player as a notification with no specifics. Retention policies for flagged audio aren't publicly detailed. The player is left reconstructing what they might have said across hours of play.

The fix: when enforcement happens, the player sees the evidence. "You were voice-restricted for this 20-second clip at 11:42 PM in Lobby #4471." Transcript shown; audio playable in the appeal flow. Flagged segments — and only flagged segments — are retained briefly, encrypted, access-logged, and deleted on a published schedule (e.g., 30 days or upon appeal resolution, whichever is later).

This single change transforms the psychology of enforcement. A punishment you can see the reason for feels like a rule. A punishment you can't feels like an ambush. And it costs the platform almost nothing: the clip already exists — a human reviewer just looked at it. The system currently shows it to everyone except the person it's about.

Principle 03 — Human-in-the-loopHuman review for serious penalties: keep it, and say so louder

Activision already does this — human moderators confirm enforcement rather than letting the AI auto-ban. It's the right call and one of the system's genuine strengths. The problem is that almost no players know it. The perceived experience is "the AI banned me," which corrodes trust the humans were supposed to protect.

The fix is partly design, partly communication: formalize the tiering (instant, reversible soft actions like session mutes can be automated; anything that persists across sessions requires human sign-off) and surface it in the enforcement notice itself: "This action was reviewed and confirmed by a human moderator on [date]." Trust that isn't visible doesn't function as trust.

Principle 04 — Better AI designContext-aware modeling: friends are not strangers

The highest-volume false-positive category in voice moderation is mutual banter between people who chose to play together. ToxMod already claims banter/harassment differentiation, and the pattern-over-keywords approach is the right architecture. But contextual signals available in the platform itself are stronger than anything recoverable from audio alone:

  • Relationship graph: party members and long-standing friends vs. random lobby fill. Two players with 400 shared matches yelling at each other is a different event than the same words aimed at a stranger.
  • Directionality: mutual heat vs. one-sided targeting. Harassment has a victim; banter doesn't.
  • Session trajectory: sustained escalation across a match vs. a single spike after a bad death.
  • Target response: does the other player engage back, mute, or leave? The subject's behavior is signal.

A classifier score should never travel to the policy layer naked. It should travel with a context vector, and the policy layer should weight them together. Words spoken in a pre-made squad among mutual friends should require dramatically stronger evidence to action than the same words aimed at a random.

Principle 05 — Decay systemCooling-off instead of a permanent red flag

Repeat-offender status is the right escalation concept — recidivists are the real problem population, and the 2024 enforcement changes that targeted them clearly worked. But "repeat offender" must be a state a player can exit. If a flag from eighteen months ago still shades how the system treats you, you've built a caste system: players who've been marked and players who haven't, forever.

The fix is a decaying score. Every clean session decays it; it can reach zero; players can see it. ("Your account standing: Good. Last incident: 14 months ago — no longer affecting your status.") Xbox's enforcement-strike system already gestures at this direction with expiring strikes. The published CoD data itself supports it — if 80% of warned players never re-offend, the system's own numbers say most flagged players respond to correction. Treat them like it.

Design rule: the system's memory of a player should be as forgiving as it expects the player to become.

Principle 06 — User-side safetyPlayer controls baked in: safety is not one-size-fits-all

Global enforcement answers "what does the platform tolerate?" A separate, equally important question is "what does this player want to hear?" — and it has different answers for a 13-year-old, a competitive adult squad, and a streamer. Player-side controls handle it without invoking the ban hammer:

  • Category auto-mute: "mute strangers by default, always keep my squad."
  • Filter strictness tiers: strict defaults for child accounts, relaxed for verified adults who opt in.
  • Personal block lists that stay personal: blocking someone removes them from your experience without feeding the global enforcement pipeline. Curation and punishment are different tools.

Every heated moment resolved by a player-side control is an incident the enforcement pipeline never has to adjudicate. Player agency isn't a concession to the anti-moderation crowd — it's load-shedding for the whole system.

Architecture

How the pieces fit

Nothing below requires inventing new detection technology. It's a policy and product layer wrapped around classifiers that already exist.

Capture & process

  • Real-time voice → speech-to-text (already deployed at CoD scale)
  • Lightweight tone/emotion analysis on the audio channel
  • Flagged segments only persisted — encrypted at rest, access-logged, TTL-enforced deletion on a published retention schedule
  • Non-flagged audio is never stored beyond the processing window

Model stack — specialists, not a monolith

Separate classifiers, separately tunable and separately auditable:

  • Slurs / hate speech — highest precision requirement, lowest tolerance for misses
  • Threats — including the real-harm categories (violence, doxxing) that warrant escalation beyond in-game penalties
  • Harassment patterns — sustained, directional targeting across a session
  • Heat level — tone, volume, repetition; a modulating signal, not an offense class by itself

Each emits a score plus the context vector from Principle 04 (relationship, directionality, trajectory, target response).

Policy layer — the rules engine

This is where the six principles become code:

  • Maps (score, context, history) → action: warn → session mute → temp voice restriction → temp ban → escalation to human review
  • Applies decay continuously; account standing is a first-class, player-visible value
  • Differentiates first-offense from pattern; hard-codes the human-in-the-loop gate for any persistent penalty
  • Every action emits an evidence record (clip reference, transcript, rule invoked, reviewer ID hash) — the artifact that powers the appeal flow

Critically, the policy layer is legible. When the rules are code with named thresholds instead of tribal moderator knowledge, you can publish them (Principle 01), audit them for disparate outcomes, and tune them without retraining a model.

Appeals & feedback — the trust loop

  • Enforcement notice shows the evidence: rule cited, clip, timestamp, human-review confirmation
  • One-tap structured appeal: "This was banter with friends" / "Wrong speaker attributed" / "Context missing" — plus free text
  • Appeal outcomes feed back as labeled training data: sustained appeals are the highest-value false-positive labels the system will ever get, and today they're mostly discarded into support-ticket queues
  • Publish appeal volume and overturn rates in the existing transparency reports. Ubisoft already publishes false-positive rates; the precedent exists.

That last point deserves emphasis. An appeals system isn't just fairness theater — it's a free, self-improving labeling pipeline for exactly the errors the models most need to learn from, generated by the people most motivated to surface them.

The hard part

The tradeoffs, honestly

Any proposal that pretends these don't exist should be ignored, including this one.

Privacy vs. evidence. Fair enforcement requires a record; respecting players requires not hoarding one. The resolution is scope and retention discipline: store only flagged segments, encrypt them, log every access, publish the deletion schedule, and delete on it. "We record everything forever" and "we can show you nothing" are both trust failures — the defensible position is a narrow, auditable evidence window.

Safety vs. freedom. Protecting vulnerable players and preserving anything-goes trash talk are in genuine tension, and no tuning eliminates it. The honest move is to choose a balance and say what it is — publish the line (Principle 01), give individuals control over their own experience beyond it (Principle 06), and stop pretending the tension away. Players forgive a rule they disagree with far more readily than a rule they can't locate.

Speed vs. accuracy. Real-time action will always be less accurate than deliberate review. The resolution is tiering: instant actions must be soft and reversible (session-scoped mutes); anything that persists must be slow enough for a human. Latency is a feature when the penalty is permanent.

Cost. Evidence storage, appeal handling, human review at tier — none of this is free. But weigh it against what opacity already costs: player churn from perceived-unjust enforcement (Activision's own case study ties moderation quality to retention), support load from evidence-free appeals that resolve nothing, and community-wide cynicism that suppresses voice chat usage entirely. The 23% actionable-report figure is what an evidence-starved process costs today, in production, at scale.

The case

Why the platform should want this

Set aside fairness for a moment and consider pure self-interest:

  1. Retention. Activision's published analysis already connects reduced toxicity exposure to improved player engagement. The same logic applies to enforcement experience: players who understand why they were actioned and can see a path back to good standing don't rage-quit the franchise. Perceived injustice churns players just as surely as harassment does.
  2. Cheaper, better appeals. Evidence-backed appeals resolve in minutes instead of ticket-thread weeks, and their outcomes are model training data instead of support overhead.
  3. Regulatory positioning. The industry trend line is unambiguous — Xbox's semi-annual transparency reports, EA's transparency reporting, Ubisoft publishing false-positive rates, and the EU's Digital Services Act normalizing statement-of-reasons requirements for content decisions. Evidence, explanation, and appeal are becoming table stakes. Building the fair version now is cheaper than retrofitting it under mandate later.
  4. The moderation story becomes a marketing asset. Activision is already unusually transparent with aggregate data. The next differentiator isn't another exposure-reduction percentage — it's being the first major shooter where players trust the referee. In a genre where every competitor's moderation is a black box, "we show you the clip" is a headline feature.

Closing

Design problems can be shipped

Call of Duty's voice moderation is a genuine technical achievement wrapped in a process players experience as arbitrary. The detection stack — ToxMod's pattern recognition, human-confirmed enforcement, published aggregate results — is ahead of most of the industry. What's missing is everything that happens around a flag: the player's right to see the evidence, a memory that forgives, context that distinguishes friends from targets, rules specific enough to follow, and controls that let players shape their own experience.

None of that requires better AI. It requires deciding that the system's job isn't only to catch toxicity — it's to be trusted while doing so. A moderation system players believe in gets something no enforcement pipeline can force: voluntary cooperation. Players who trust the referee report accurately, appeal honestly, and self-correct after a warning — the published data shows they already do that last one 80% of the time.

The detection problem is solved. The trust problem is a design problem. Design problems can be shipped.
